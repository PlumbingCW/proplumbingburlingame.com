How to Replace a Water Heater Pressure Relief Valve

The temperature and pressure relief valve on a water heater is replaceable with basic tools, but only after you shut off the energy source, close the cold supply and relieve the pressure in the tank. If the valve is dripping, test it before you replace it, because a drip often points to a pressure problem, not a bad valve.

This guide covers the steps, the mistakes that cause scalds and leaks, and the point where a Burlingame plumber should take over.

What the Valve Does and Why It Must Never Be Blocked

A relief valve is the safety device that opens if the water in the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. It lets water out through a discharge pipe so the tank cannot burst. It is the one part on a water heater that you never cap, plug or remove.

The valve has a small lever on the front. Lifting it briefly should release a burst of water through the discharge pipe, and releasing it should seal again. A valve that stays open, or one that never opens, is a failed valve.

Because the valve guards against a dangerous condition, a replacement must be rated for your tank, with the same pressure and temperature rating printed on the tank label.

Is the Valve Bad or Is the System Overpressured

A valve that drips every day is not always faulty. Water expands when it heats, and in a closed system, with a pressure regulator or a check valve at the meter, the extra volume has nowhere to go. Pressure rises each time the heater fires, and the relief valve opens a little to protect the tank.

The cure is a thermal expansion tank on the cold line, not a new valve. A new valve in an overpressured system will start dripping within weeks.

Step by Step: Replacing the Valve

Shut off the energy

Turn the gas control to pilot or off, or switch off the breaker for an electric heater. Let the water cool for a while.

Close the cold supply

Turn the cold water valve above the heater clockwise until it stops.

Relieve the pressure

Open a hot tap upstairs and lift the valve lever to release pressure. Drain a few gallons from the drain valve so water level falls below the valve port.

Remove the old valve

Take off the discharge pipe, then unscrew the valve with a pipe wrench, turning counterclockwise.

Wrap the threads of the new valve with a thread sealant approved for potable water, then thread it in by hand and snug it with the wrench. Do not overtighten. Reconnect the discharge pipe, open the cold valve and a hot tap and let the tank fill until water runs steadily from the tap. Then restore power or gas.

Check for leaks at the valve and watch it for the next hour.

The Discharge Pipe Matters as Much as the Valve

The pipe that carries water away from the valve has rules. It should be the same size as the valve outlet or larger, run downhill and end in a visible, safe place such as outdoors or a drain. It should never be capped, threaded at the end or fitted with a valve.

Hot water coming out of the end could scald someone, so keep the outlet away from walkways. If the old discharge pipe is cracked or crusted, replace it at the same time.

Related Water Heater Safety in Burlingame

California requires water heaters to be braced or strapped against earthquake movement under Health and Safety Code section 19211, and a leaning or loose heater can strain the valve and the connections. Check the straps while you are there.

If the heater is old, with rust at the base or a tank that has been dripping for years, replacing the valve will not fix the underlying problem. See water heater replacement if the tank looks tired.

Standards Behind the Discharge Line

The California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), sets rules for how a relief valve discharge line is run, including its size, its slope and where it ends. That is why a capped, threaded or valved discharge line is never acceptable, whatever a previous owner did.

Know your limits

When to Call a Plumber

  • The valve will not budge or the fitting is corroded and risks breaking.
  • The valve drips after replacement, which points to high pressure.
  • You do not have a thermal expansion tank and the system is closed.
  • The tank is leaking, rusting or very old.
  • You are not comfortable working with gas or hot water.
