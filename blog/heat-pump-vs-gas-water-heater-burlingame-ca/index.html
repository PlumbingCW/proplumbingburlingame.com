Heat Pump vs Gas Water Heater: What the 2028 Rule Means

A heat pump water heater uses far less energy than a gas tank, but it needs space for air, an electrical circuit and a higher upfront price. A gas tank costs less to buy and install today. The Bay Area Air District delayed its zero-NOx standard for gas water heaters in 2026, so a gas replacement is still allowed for now.

This guide compares the two, explains the rule and lists what to check before you choose.

By the Burlingame Pro Plumbing team | Updated November 16, 2026

What the Air District Decided

In 2026, the Bay Area Air District board voted 16 to 1 to delay the zero-NOx standard for gas water heaters from January 2027 to January 2028. The standard is part of Rule 9-6, and it applies when you buy a new water heater. Most heat pump water heaters meet it, and standard gas tanks do not.

The rule does not require you to replace a working heater. It also has exemptions for lower-income households, for tanks of 30 gallons or less and for larger units until 2033.

  • Households with income at or below 80 percent of area median income.
  • Households on Medi-Cal or CalFresh.
  • Households whose housing cost is at least 28 percent of income.
  • A one-time exemption for physical or electrical barriers.

Rules can change again, so confirm with the Air District or your installer before you buy.

Space and Electrical Needs

A heat pump pulls heat from the air around it, so it needs a room with enough volume or a ducted setup. A garage is the common location. A tight closet can work with ducts, and a small closet without airflow is a poor fit.

The unit usually needs a 240-volt circuit and a spot in the panel. If your panel is full or old, an upgrade may be needed. We check panel capacity and clearance on a site visit before we recommend a model.

Burlingame Permits and Incentives

A fee waiver worth knowing

Burlingame waives its permit fee when a licensed contractor replaces a gas water heater or furnace with a heat pump. State fees still apply. The permit comes through the Building Division, and we handle it.

California Health and Safety Code section 19211 requires strapping on new and replacement heaters, and a heat pump tank must be braced like any other.

Costs That Add Up

A heat pump costs more to buy and install, and the savings come from lower running costs over the years. How long it takes to break even depends on your gas and electric rates and on your hot water use.

Utility rebates and programs may reduce the cost, and they change often. Ask your installer and check with your utility for current offers. Do not buy based on a rebate you have not confirmed.

Questions to Ask Before You Choose

  • Will it fit in my space with enough air flow?
  • Does my electrical panel have capacity or need an upgrade?
  • What are the current rebates and are they confirmed?
  • Does my household use enough hot water to justify the cost?
  • What is the plan if I replace only after the old one fails?

We install both types. Our water heater replacement page covers the swap, and our water heater installation page covers a new install. For on-demand options, see tankless water heaters.

A Walk Through a Typical Decision

Imagine a family of four in a 1950s Burlingame house with a gas tank in the garage that is about to fail. The garage has room, the panel has a free slot and the family wants lower bills. A heat pump is a strong candidate, and the permit fee is waived.

Now imagine a condo with a heater in a small closet and a full panel. A heat pump may not fit without ducting and a panel upgrade, and a gas or electric tank may be the practical choice. The 2028 date does not force anyone to act, so there is time to plan.

The best approach is to look at your space and panel before the tank fails. We can do that in a short visit so you know your options while the old tank is still working.

What Happens After 2028

If the standard takes effect as scheduled in January 2028, a new gas tank that does not meet the zero-NOx limit will not be sold for installation in the Bay Area, apart from the exemptions. Existing gas heaters can stay in service and be repaired. Stocking and sales rules apply to new units.

That makes the next few years a window in which a standard gas replacement is still allowed. It is not a reason to rush into a purchase you do not want, and it is not a reason to wait if your tank fails. Plan for either and keep your options open.

Rules may change, so check the current status with the Air District or your installer at the time you buy.
