When Repiping Is the Better Value

A single leak is a repair. A pattern of leaks is a signal that the pipe is failing along its length. If you have had two or three repairs in a short time, the next one is already forming.

Multiple pinhole leaks

Corrosion from inside the pipe is attacking several spots at once.

Rusty or brown water

Galvanized steel rusting from the inside colors the water.

Falling pressure over the years

Mineral and rust buildup narrows old pipe.

Repeated repairs on one line

The cost of patching adds up to more than a plan.

What We Check Before Recommending Repiping

We start with a pressure test and a look at the pipe material at every accessible point. Galvanized steel, early copper and some polybutylene runs fail in different ways, and the plan depends on which you have.

Burlingame has many older homes, and the age of the home tells us a lot. We also check incoming pressure, since high pressure shortens the life of any pipe. The city runs pressure-reducing valves and booster pumps above 120 feet in elevation, so a regulator is often part of a repipe. A smaller problem may only need leak detection and a repair of a single run.

How a Repipe Is Done

Plan and schedule

We map the new lines, choose the pipe material and agree on the days we will be in your home.

Open small access points

We use small openings in walls and ceilings so patching is limited.

Install new lines

New copper or PEX replaces old pipe, with new shutoffs and connections at each fixture.

Test, inspect and patch

We pressure test, meet the inspector and close the access holes.

Most homes are done in a few days, and we try to keep the water on at the end of each day.

Replacement piping must be a material approved for drinking water under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and the Building Division inspects the finished work.

Copper or PEX

Pipe material comparison
MaterialStrengthsKeep in mind
CopperLong service life, familiar, heat tolerant.Higher material cost.

Burlingame water is soft and alkaline, and it does not attack copper in the way some water does. Either material can work well here. We explain which fits your house and your budget.

Planning Around Your Walls and Your Budget

A repipe does not need to happen all at once. In some houses we replace the worst runs first, such as the kitchen and hall bath lines, and come back for the rest during a later remodel. That spreads the cost, and it avoids opening finished walls twice.

We also tell you where to leave pipe alone. Copper lines that are in good shape and test sound do not need to be replaced just because the neighbor repiped. A fair assessment saves money.

If you are remodeling a bath or kitchen, it is the best time to replace the supply lines behind the walls. See plumbing installation for how we coordinate with contractors.
