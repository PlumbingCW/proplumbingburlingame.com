Toilet Leaking at the Base: Wax Ring Replacement

Water around the bottom of a toilet most often means the wax ring between the bowl and the drain flange has failed. The fix is to shut off the water, lift the toilet, replace the ring and reset the bowl on a sound flange.

Before you pull the toilet, confirm the water is not coming from somewhere else, because a sweating tank or a loose supply line can look the same.

By the Burlingame Pro Plumbing team | Updated November 16, 2026

Is It the Wax Ring or Something Else

Dry the floor and base with a towel, then look closely. Water that returns after each flush and spreads from under the bowl points to the seal. Water that appears on the floor but drips from the tank or the supply line points elsewhere.

Where the water comes from
What you noticeLikely source
Wet around the base after flushingWax ring or flange.
Drips from the tank bottom boltsTank-to-bowl gasket or bolts.
Water at the supply connectionLoose or worn supply line.
Cold tank beads with water in summerCondensation, not a leak.
Toilet rocks on the floorLoose bolts or flange problem.

A sewer smell near the toilet also points to a failed seal, since the ring blocks gas as well as water.

Why the Ring Fails

The wax ring compresses when the toilet is set and stays sealed for years. It fails when the toilet moves, when the flange sits too low or is broken or when the ring has simply aged. A toilet that rocks slightly every day breaks the seal over time.

Water that leaks from the ring does not stay on the floor. It soaks into the subfloor, and by the time you notice a stain, the wood may be rotting. That is why a base leak is worth fixing promptly.

How Replacement Works

Shut off and drain

Close the shutoff valve, flush, and sponge out the tank and bowl.

Disconnect and lift

Remove the supply line and the nuts on the closet bolts, then lift the toilet straight up. It is heavy, so ask for help.

Clean and inspect the flange

Scrape off the old wax and look for cracks, rust or a flange that sits below the floor.

Set a new ring and reseat

Place the new ring, lower the toilet straight down and press firmly, then tighten bolts evenly.

Check the flange height. A flange that sits below the finished floor may need a spacer ring or a thicker seal. Tighten the nuts in turns, so the porcelain does not crack.

Reconnect the supply, open the valve and flush several times, watching for leaks at the base.

When the Floor or Flange Is Damaged

If the subfloor feels spongy, the flange is cracked or the toilet will not sit level, a new wax ring alone will not hold. Flange repair kits and subfloor patches are common in older bathrooms, and doing it right matters, because the next leak will start the same way.

If water has been leaking for some time, check the ceiling below and the room beside the bathroom for stains. A wider leak may need leak detection to be sure the water is only coming from the toilet.

Burlingame Homes and Toilet Replacements

Many Burlingame homes have older toilets that use 3 gallons or more per flush. The city's Indoor Water Conservation Ordinance sets toilets at 1.28 gallons per flush or less for permitted new construction and for kitchen and bath remodels, and a replacement toilet in an older home saves water too.

If the toilet is old, cracked or constantly clogging, replacing it may be better than a ring. Our toilet repair team can do either, and our plumbing installation crew handles a full bathroom update.

Tools and Materials for the Job

  • A new wax ring or wax-free seal, with a horn if your flange needs it.
  • New closet bolts and washers.
  • A putty knife for scraping old wax.
  • Gloves, rags and a plastic bag for the old ring.
  • A wrench or pliers for the supply line and nuts.
  • A sponge and a bucket for the remaining water.
  • Shims to level a toilet on an uneven floor.

A toilet weighs enough to hurt your back, so have a helper and lift with your legs. Place the bowl on an old towel or cardboard while the flange is exposed. Stuff a rag in the drain to block sewer gas while you work and remember to remove it before setting the toilet.

A wax ring cannot be reused. Once compressed, it will not reseal, and a second use is the most common reason for a repeat leak.

Setting the Toilet Straight and True

Set the toilet straight down onto the bolts without twisting it. A twisting motion smears the wax and breaks the seal. Press down firmly and sit on it for a moment to compress the ring. Then add washers and nuts and tighten a little at a time, alternating sides.

Stop when the toilet is firmly seated. Overtightening cracks the base and is a leading cause of a leak after a repair. Shim the low side if the toilet rocks, rather than cranking the nuts down.

After caulking around the base for a finished look, leave a small gap at the back so any future leak shows. Some plumbers skip caulk at the back for that reason.

Burlingame toilets and the water conservation rules

Burlingame has an Indoor Water Conservation Ordinance that sets toilets at 1.28 gallons per flush for permitted new construction and kitchen and bath remodels. If a remodel in your house included a new toilet, the replacement has to meet that limit.

A new wax ring does not change the flush rating. If the floor is soft around the base, call before reseating the bowl, because rot under the flange needs repair first.

What the Code Expects of a Toilet Setting

The California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), expects a toilet to sit on a firmly fixed flange with a watertight seal. A flange that has rusted, cracked or dropped below the floor needs repair before a new seal can do its job, which is why we inspect it every time we lift a toilet.
